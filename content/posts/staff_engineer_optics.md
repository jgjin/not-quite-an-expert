In _Staff Engineer_, Will Larson observes:
> Suppose you're interviewing for a new role twenty years into your career. Will the folks interviewing you understand your real impact on any of your previous projects or companies? No, I guarantee they won't. Instead, you'll find yourself judged by a series of surprisingly subjective measures: your accumulated prestige, the titles you've had and companies you've worked at, your backchannel reputation, and how you present in your interview process.

Let's merge "your accumulated prestige" into "the titles you've had and companies you've worked at", since "your accumulated prestige" feels too general and the titles and companies probably form the bulk of your accumulated prestige. I'm simplifying your programs and schools as part of your titles and companies, since in my experience they are treated similarly for applications.

Then for applications, I'd order:
1. who you know
2. your titles and companies
3. your image from your interviews
4. your reputation

Who you know depends on where you grew up and where you've been. Recognizing little control of where you grew up and avoiding the cycle between where you've been and where you're applying to be, we'll drop "who you know".

We'll also drop "your reputation". Unless you do something egregious which becomes public, you'll have a good enough reputation. Larson comments:
> [R]eferences and backchannel reference checks are deeply random. If you need any further evidence of that, look to the serial harassers who continue to get hired job after job at prominent companies.

So your titles and companies get you into position and your image from your interviews gets you through. Larson optimistically proposes:
> You can't escape subjective interview practices, but you can deliberately accumulate expertise from doing valuable work. Indeed, that's the only viable long‐term bet on your career: focus on work that matters, do projects that develop you, and steer towards companies that value genuine experience.

However, other parts of the book suggest otherwise. Becoming visible to leadership, staying aligned with authority, activating an executive sponsor - Larson lists these as important activities for a Staff Engineer, though it's hard to see them as building valuable systems for users. And if "subjective interview practices" didn't make it clear, when Larson asks
> Do you interview well?

it's clear interviewing is a skill also distinct from building valuable systems. Against the substance of building valuable systems, some of the activities for getting the title and some of the skill of interviewing are [optics]({{< ref "hwyl_optics.md" >}}).

To be fair, getting the title and interviewing well both require a baseline competence. However, among those who have the title or interview well, I've seen a massive variance in their actual capability to build valuable systems.

Then what to do? At the individual level, we should recognize these optics as influential to our career outcomes, and engage with them to the degree we feel comfortable, to some intuitive limit. At the company level, we should tune our recruiting against shallow affinity instead toward a grounded representation of the job. I've appreciated practical coding interviews over algorithmic ones, and current production and previous project system design interviews over hypothetical ones. At each level, we should earnestly adapt.
